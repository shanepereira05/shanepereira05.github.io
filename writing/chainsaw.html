Every PM got a chainsaw. Now what?

A year of AI across my PM team: where it worked, where we sawed trees to make toothpicks, and the habit that fixed it.

AI Frameworks Team Discovery
Shane Pereira
Lead Technical Product Manager

So early 2026, along with the rest of the world, our organization encouraged us to use AI in our work. Just like most companies, we too had invested heavily into AI and every employee now had access to a hypothetical chainsaw (dramatising for effect). I am going to draw a similarity between AI and a chainsaw. There will be a lot of puns along the way and some of them are intentional.

Just like a chainsaw, we had no idea as to what and how this would work. See, as Product Managers, we are more like carpenters and less like a lumberjack. We carve out the needs and hammer in the details of our customers so they could use that as a stepping stool to achieve their goals. With chainsaws in our hands and a forest in the views, we set out to carve our needs.

Step 1: Direction

The possibilities are endless and there is a high chance that we don’t see the forest for the trees. We needed guidance on what we could do with such a heavy-duty tool. We sat together and decided to start off with the one thing that’s marginalised the most, the instruction manual, AKA documentation. AI gave us the opportunity to get creative with documentation and since most of us were handed over a product which was new, it gave us an opportunity to learn while we go. Soon came requests to have MCP servers for Confluence, JIRA and Git, which were fulfilled unlocking a world where logic could be checked against code.

Step 2: Outcome

With a series of message exchanges, we started carving out documentation in a way where bringing simplicity through images, simulations and interactive input/output cases became a bare minimum in documentation. Multilingual training documents helped unlock a new set of customers. Adoption grew higher and Confluence started to look a lot cleaner.

Step 3: Refine

What we hadn’t kept a close eye on was context.

We were sawing trees to make toothpicks.

Where we could optimise was in foreseeing what is needed and guiding the agent to achieve exactly that. A lot like how our customers would feel when describing what they need. As PMs, being able to describe the problem, define the guardrails, and know exactly what the end result would be is something that should come naturally, but getting there is a process.

The fix was embarrassingly simple: mind-map before you prompt. The tool goes to work when you can describe the cut not every time your hand reaches for it.

Step 4: Next cut

What comes next is a roadmap. You now have the big picture that shows you where there is opportunity and improvements to the product. Build that into a roadmap and feed it as context. Agents do a good job of keeping you honest when they have complete context of what you are trying to achieve and also sidetrack less.

A chainsaw was designed to replace the axe, not the lumberjack.
